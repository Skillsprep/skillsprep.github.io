DMAIC phases with a worked example

DMAIC is the five-phase problem-solving structure of Six Sigma, used when an existing process performs badly and the cause is not yet known: Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve, Control. The worked example below is illustrative; the figures are invented to show the logic.

Scenario: a service team notices that many customer orders need correction after entry.

PhasePurposeExample activity
DefineClarify the problem, scope, customer need and goalCharter: "Reduce order-entry errors in the online order team". Name the sponsor and the customer requirement: correct orders on first entry.
MeasureEstablish a baseline with reliable dataCount orders checked and orders with errors for four weeks; confirm everyone defines "error" the same way. Suppose 8 of every 100 orders contain an error.
AnalyzeFind and verify root causesSort errors by type with a Pareto chart; find most come from free-text address fields. Use 5-Whys to confirm.
ImproveTest and implement solutionsPilot dropdown fields and automatic address checks; compare error rates with the baseline.
ControlHold the gainsUpdate the procedure, train staff, add a control chart and name an owner who reviews it monthly.

The key discipline is that solutions come after data and root cause, never before.

Each phase normally ends with a review, sometimes called a tollgate, where the sponsor and team confirm the work is complete before moving on. In the example, the team would not leave Define until the problem statement, scope and goal were agreed, and would not start Improve until the Analyze phase had verified, with data, that address-field entry really caused most errors. Skipping these checks is how teams end up solving the wrong problem. Also note that DMAIC is not always strictly linear: findings in Analyze may send the team back to Measure for better data.

The eight wastes (DOWNTIME), 5S and Kaizen

Lean defines waste as any activity that consumes resources without adding value the customer would pay for. The eight wastes are remembered by the acronym DOWNTIME:

LetterWasteWorkplace example
DDefectsInvoices that must be corrected and reissued
OOverproductionPrinting 500 forms when 100 are needed
WWaitingStaff idle until an approval arrives
NNon-utilized talentA skilled analyst only doing data entry
TTransportationMoving materials or files between distant sites without need
IInventoryExcess stock or a growing backlog of unprocessed requests
MMotionWalking to a distant printer or searching for tools
EExtra-processingEntering the same data into two systems

5S organises the workplace in five steps: Sort (remove what is not needed), Set in order (a place for everything), Shine (clean and inspect), Standardize (make the rules visible and consistent) and Sustain (build the habit and audit it).

Kaizen means continuous improvement through many small changes made by the people doing the work. A kaizen event is a short, focused team effort on one process, but the idea is a daily habit, not a one-off project.

Value-stream mapping, takt time, PDCA and DMAIC

A value-stream map shows every step that turns a request or material into a delivered product, with the information flow that controls it. For each step you record process time, waiting time, and often inventory or queue size. Steps are then classed as value-added, necessary non-value-added, or waste. Maps are drawn for the current state first, then a future state that removes the waste.

Takt time is the pace of production needed to meet customer demand: available working time divided by customer demand. If a team has 450 available minutes per day and customers need 90 units, takt time is 450 / 90 = 5 minutes per unit.

PDCA (Plan, Do, Check, Act) is a four-step loop for continuous improvement: plan a change, try it on a small scale, check the results, then adopt or adjust it. DMAIC is a more structured five-phase method with heavy use of data and statistics, suited to problems whose cause is unknown or complex.

5-Whys versus the fishbone diagram

Both tools look for root causes. 5-Whys follows a single chain of causes by asking "why?" repeatedly; five is a guide, not a rule. It is fast and works best for simple problems with one likely path.

Example: a delivery was late. Why? The parcel missed the courier pickup. Why? Packing finished late. Why? The label printer was not working. Why? It ran out of ribbon. Why? No one owns reordering supplies. The root cause is a missing ownership rule, not a slow packer.

A fishbone (Ishikawa or cause-and-effect) diagram explores many possible causes at once. The problem is the fish head; the main bones are categories such as Methods, Machines, Materials, People, Measurement and Environment, and the team brainstorms causes under each. It suits complex problems with several contributing factors. The usual sequence is to use a fishbone to map possibilities, verify the likely ones with data, and then use 5-Whys to drill into the most probable branch. A common 5-Whys mistake is stopping at a person ("the packer was careless"); a good root cause usually points to a process, rule or system that can be fixed. Another is accepting an answer without evidence, so check each "because" against facts.

Sustainability pillars and stakeholder engagement

The triple bottom line asks organisations to measure success on three dimensions: social (people), environmental (planet) and economic (profit or prosperity). Lean and sustainability overlap, because removing waste usually cuts energy, materials and cost, but the framing is broader. Good projects state one goal for each pillar, or explain why one does not apply, and avoid improving one by quietly harming another.

Stakeholders are the people and groups who affect or are affected by the work: customers, employees, suppliers, regulators, communities and owners. Practical tips:

  • Identify and map: list stakeholders by their influence and interest to decide how closely to involve each.
  • Engage early: involving people at the start builds ownership and surfaces information you would miss.
  • Communicate regularly and openly: share progress, results and setbacks in plain language.
  • Listen and respond: record concerns and show what changed because of them.
  • Define the goal first: a clear project goal and charter keep discussions focused.
Exam habit When two answers look plausible, pick the one that starts with defining the goal and involving stakeholders early.

Question 1 of 30Process Improvement

Your team project is consistently running late. How can the 5-Whys technique be applied to this situation?

Correct answer: Ask why the project is running late multiple times until pinpointing poor initial planning as the root cause
5-Whys drills down through successive 'why' questions to the root cause instead of treating symptoms.
Question 2 of 30Process Improvement

Why is it important to align process improvements with organizational objectives?

Correct answer: Aligning process improvements with organizational objectives ensures that changes contribute to the wider goals and enhance overall effectiveness.
Improvements that ladder up to organizational goals actually move the business forward.
Question 3 of 30Process Improvement

You are reviewing your team's ongoing project. How should you involve stakeholders in the planning process to improve overall outcomes?

Correct answer: Engage stakeholders early to surface valuable information and foster ownership.
Early engagement uncovers insights and builds buy-in — approval-only involvement comes too late.
Question 4 of 30Process Improvement

What is the primary benefit of identifying value-added activities in a business process?

Correct answer: It helps in focusing efforts on tasks that directly impact customer satisfaction and product quality.
Value-added analysis concentrates effort where it matters to the customer.
Question 5 of 30Process Improvement

You are leading a project to transition to new technology in your department. What should you prioritize to minimize downtime and ensure a smooth implementation?

Correct answer: Provide necessary training and resources for the team.
Trained, resourced teams adopt new technology with minimal disruption.
