Project Management

Project Management Study Guide: Concepts That Matter

14 min read 20 Questions Published Aug 22, 2026 Reviewed Oct 18, 2026

Unofficial study resource. Written independently of any exam provider, employer or software vendor.

Project management interviews and skills tests mix methodology knowledge with scenario judgment. Unlike coding topics, many answers hinge on choosing the best-practice response rather than a strictly right one.

Methodology knowledge

Be crystal clear on the differences:

AspectAgile/ScrumWaterfall
PlanningIncremental, per sprintUpfront, comprehensive
RequirementsExpected to evolveFixed at start
DeliveryWorking increments earlyFinal product at end
Best forUncertain/changing scopeStable, regulated scope

Know Scrum roles: Product Owner (prioritizes backlog), Scrum Master (removes impediments, facilitates), and the Developers (self-managing; older editions of the Scrum Guide called them the Development Team).

Key frameworks to recognize

  • Iron Triangle — Scope, Time, Cost (with Quality in the center). Changing one affects the others.
  • RACI matrix — Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed. Exactly one A per task.
  • Critical Path — the longest sequence of dependent tasks; it determines the project's minimum duration.
  • Risk matrix — Probability × Impact. High/high risks get mitigation plans.
  • Triple constraint trade-offs — scenario: deadline moved up → cut scope or add resources.

Scenario question strategy

Many scenario questions describe a project problem and ask what the PM should do first. Best-practice answers usually follow this order:

  1. Analyze/assess the situation (gather facts, check impact)
  2. Communicate with stakeholders
  3. Then act (escalate, change plan, add resources)
Pattern Answers that immediately escalate to management or blame team members are rarely the best first step. Answers starting with analysis or communication are usually the better choice.

Scrum, Waterfall and Kanban: choosing by the shape of the work

Interviewers rarely want a definition of each method. They want to hear when you would pick one. Waterfall completes phases in order (requirements, design, build, test, release) and suits work where scope is stable and changes are expensive, such as building a bridge. Scrum delivers in short cycles and suits work where learning from early results should change what you build next. Kanban has no fixed cycle at all: work flows continuously across a board and you limit how many items sit in each column.

The 2020 Scrum Guide describes a single Scrum Team with no sub-teams or hierarchy. It contains three accountabilities:

  • Product Owner: maximises the value of the product and manages the Product Backlog.
  • Scrum Master: helps the team and the organisation understand and use Scrum; a coach, not a manager.
  • Developers: the people who create any part of a usable Increment each Sprint.

The five events are the Sprint (a container for all the others, one month or less), Sprint Planning, the Daily Scrum (15 minutes), the Sprint Review and the Sprint Retrospective. There are three artifacts, each paired with a commitment: the Product Backlog with the Product Goal, the Sprint Backlog with the Sprint Goal, and the Increment with the Definition of Done.

SituationBetter fitWhy
Fixed regulatory scope, signed-off specificationWaterfallLittle to learn along the way
New product, unclear what users wantScrumEach Sprint gives feedback
Support requests arriving dailyKanbanContinuous flow, no planning cycle needed
Common mistake Calling the Scrum Master the team's boss, or treating the Daily Scrum as a status report to the manager. It is a planning meeting for the Developers.

Critical path and float: a worked example

Take six tasks. Durations are in days.

TaskDurationPredecessors
A3none
B4A
C2A
D5B
E3C
F2D, E

List every path from start to finish and add the durations. A-B-D-F = 3+4+5+2 = 14. A-C-E-F = 3+2+3+2 = 10. The longest path is the critical path, so the project takes at least 14 days and tasks A, B, D and F have zero float.

Float is how long a task can slip without delaying the finish. Task E can start at day 5 at the earliest (after A and C). F cannot begin until D finishes at day 12, so E may finish as late as day 12 instead of day 8. Its total float is 12 - 8 = 4 days. Task C also has 4 days of float, but that float is shared: if C uses all 4, E has none left.

  • Delay on a critical task delays the project day for day.
  • To shorten the project you must shorten a critical task, and the critical path may then switch to the other route.
  • Crashing adds resources to critical tasks and raises cost. Fast tracking overlaps tasks and raises risk.
Interview reasoning Asked "what if B slips by 2 days?", answer: critical task, so the end moves from day 14 to day 16. Asked the same about E, answer: absorbed by float, no change.

Earned value worked example and the control documents

Project: budget at completion (BAC) of 100,000 over 10 weeks, spread evenly. At the end of week 4 you review. Planned value (PV) is 4/10 of BAC = 40,000. The team has finished work that was budgeted at 30,000, so earned value (EV) = 30,000. Actual cost (AC) is 36,000.

MeasureCalculationResultReading
CPIEV / AC = 30,000 / 36,0000.83Each unit spent returns 0.83 of work
SPIEV / PV = 30,000 / 40,0000.75Behind schedule
CVEV - AC-6,000Over budget
SVEV - PV-10,000Behind plan

If you assume the current cost efficiency continues, EAC = BAC / CPI = 100,000 / 0.8333 = 120,000. Below 1 is bad for both indexes; above 1 is good. A frequent slip is dividing the wrong way round, so remember that EV is always the numerator.

RACI clarifies who does what. For the task "Approve the budget": Finance Lead = R (Responsible, does the work), Sponsor = A (Accountable, one person only, signs off), Project Manager = C (Consulted), Team = I (Informed). A common error is two As on one row, which means nobody truly owns the decision.

A risk register row holds the risk, probability, impact, a score (probability multiplied by impact), a response (avoid, mitigate, transfer, accept), an owner and a status. Write risks as "Because of X, Y may happen, causing Z", not as a vague worry. An issue has already happened; a risk has not.

Stakeholders, change control and how to practise

Stakeholder analysis often uses a power and interest grid. High power, high interest: manage closely. High power, low interest: keep satisfied. Low power, high interest: keep informed. Low power, low interest: monitor. The grid is a starting point; revisit it, because a stakeholder's position changes during a project.

Scenario: midway through, a sponsor asks for an extra report. Do not simply say yes or no. A sound sequence is: record the request, assess its impact on scope, schedule, cost and risk, bring it to the agreed change control process (a change request, reviewed by whoever has authority, such as a change control board), then update the baselines and tell stakeholders if it is approved. Unmanaged additions are scope creep. In an Agile project the equivalent is adding the item to the Product Backlog and letting the Product Owner prioritise it, rather than inserting it into a running Sprint.

How to practise

  1. Redraw the critical path example with new durations and find the float by hand.
  2. Invent EV numbers for a small personal project and compute all four measures.
  3. Write a five-row risk register for a house move.
  4. Explain Scrum aloud in two minutes without notes.

Glossary: baseline is the approved plan used for comparison. Slack is another word for float. Increment is the usable, done result of a Sprint. Velocity is a team's observed rate of completed work, used only for its own forecasting, never to compare teams.

Common exam and interview traps

Most wrong answers in project management come from mixing up two similar ideas. Keep these pairs straight.

PairDifference
Risk and issueA risk might happen; an issue is happening now and needs action, not analysis
Product Goal and Sprint GoalThe Product Goal is the long-term target for the product; the Sprint Goal is the single purpose of one Sprint
Definition of Done and acceptance criteriaDone applies to every item and sets the quality bar; acceptance criteria are specific to one backlog item
Crashing and fast trackingCrashing adds resources (more cost); fast tracking overlaps tasks (more risk)
Responsible and AccountableResponsible does the work and may be several people; Accountable answers for the outcome and is one person

Scenario: the Sprint is halfway through and a stakeholder wants a new feature added. Weak answer: "Add it, the customer is always right." Stronger answer: the Developers own the Sprint Backlog during the Sprint, so the request goes to the Product Owner, who may order it into the Product Backlog for a later Sprint. Only if it makes the Sprint Goal obsolete should the Product Owner consider cancelling the Sprint.

Scenario: SPI is 0.75 but CPI is 1.1. The project is behind schedule yet spending efficiently, which can mean under-resourcing. A good manager asks why before choosing a fix, since adding people to a late task does not always speed it up.

Habit In any calculation question, write the formula first, substitute the numbers second, and state in words what the result means. Examiners and interviewers reward the reading as much as the arithmetic.

Practical learning exercises

  • Exercise 1 (Agile Planning): Write a user story for a login feature using the format: "As a [type of user], I want [an action] so that [a benefit/value]." Define 3 acceptance criteria for it.
  • Exercise 2 (Risk Management): Create a basic Risk Register for a hypothetical website launch. Identify 3 risks, assign them probability and impact scores (1-5), and propose a mitigation strategy for the highest-scoring risk.
  • Exercise 3 (Scheduling): Draw a simple network diagram for a 5-task project. Identify the critical path (the longest path through the network).

Practice questions

Question 1 of 20Frameworks

In a RACI matrix, how many people should be Accountable for a single task?

Why: "Accountable" means owns the outcome. With multiple A's, responsibility diffuses — the core principle of RACI is single accountability per task.
Question 2 of 20Agile

Who is responsible for prioritizing the product backlog in Scrum?

Why: The Product Owner owns the backlog and its ordering by business value. The Scrum Master facilitates the process but doesn't prioritize, and the Developers decide how much work to pull into a sprint.
Question 3 of 20Scheduling

What does the critical path of a project determine?

Why: The critical path is the longest chain of dependent tasks with zero slack. Any delay on it delays the whole project — hence it sets the minimum timeline.
Question 4 of 20Agile

In Scrum, what is the purpose of the Daily Standup?

Why: The Daily Scrum is a 15-minute event to inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal and adapt the Sprint Backlog as necessary, adjusting the upcoming planned work.
Question 5 of 20Risk Management

What is a risk register?

Why: A risk register is a repository in which outputs of risk management processes are recorded, detailing all identified risks, their probability, impact, and planned responses.
Question 6 of 20Agile

In Scrum, who is responsible for maximizing the value of the product?

Why: The Product Owner manages the backlog to ensure the team builds the most valuable features.
Question 7 of 20Scope

What is scope creep?

Why: Scope creep happens when features are added without formal approval or adjustments to the plan.
Question 8 of 20Scheduling

What does the Critical Path represent?

Why: A delay in any task on the critical path delays the entire project.
Question 9 of 20Risk

Which risk response strategy involves shifting the impact of a threat to a third party?

Why: Transferring risk often involves insurance, warranties, or outsourcing to a third party.
Question 10 of 20Stakeholders

Why is a stakeholder register important?

Why: It helps in understanding stakeholder interests and developing communication strategies.
Question 11 of 20Communication

If a project has 5 stakeholders, how many communication channels exist?

Why: 5 * (5 - 1) / 2 = 10 channels. Complexity grows rapidly with more people.
Question 12 of 20Agile

What is the purpose of the Daily Scrum (Stand-up)?

Why: It's a quick daily sync for the developers to align and plan the next 24 hours.
Question 13 of 20Cost

What does a Cost Performance Index (CPI) of 0.8 indicate?

Why: A CPI less than 1.0 indicates a cost overrun for work completed.
Question 14 of 20Quality

What is the difference between Quality Assurance (QA) and Quality Control (QC)?

Why: QA ensures the right processes are followed; QC inspects the final output.
Question 15 of 20Integration

What document formally authorizes the existence of a project?

Why: The Project Charter is signed by the sponsor and gives the PM authority.
Question 16 of 20Agile

In Kanban, what is the primary purpose of WIP (Work in Progress) limits?

Why: WIP limits force the team to finish existing work before starting new work, smoothing flow.
Question 17 of 20Procurement

Which contract type carries the most financial risk for the buyer?

Why: In Cost Reimbursable, the buyer pays all actual costs plus a fee, bearing the risk of overruns.
Question 18 of 20Closing

What is a key activity during the closing phase of a project?

Why: Capturing lessons learned ensures future projects benefit from past experiences.
Question 19 of 20Estimation

What is Three-Point Estimating?

Why: It accounts for uncertainty by looking at best, worst, and most probable scenarios.
Question 20 of 20Agile

What is a Sprint Retrospective?

Why: The retro focuses on process improvement, not product demonstration.

Final tips

  • If you have studied for PMP or CAPM, much of this will feel familiar — it covers similar ground
  • Focus on "what should the PM do FIRST" patterns
  • Brush up on the Scrum events: Sprint Planning, Daily Scrum (the standup), Sprint Review and Sprint Retrospective
