Practical AI at Work: Prompting, Checking and Using It Safely

Employers increasingly expect people to use AI assistants well: get a useful draft quickly, spot what is wrong with it, and avoid sharing what should stay private. This guide covers those habits in a tool-neutral way, so it does not depend on any one product. It is an unofficial study aid. For exam-style questions on generative AI concepts, try the Generative AI practice test.

What you will learn

By the end you should be able to:

  • Write prompts with a clear task, context, format and constraints
  • Iterate on a weak answer instead of accepting or abandoning it
  • Check facts, figures, references and summaries before you rely on them
  • Decide what you should and should not paste into an AI tool
  • Explain, at overview level, the NIST AI Risk Management Framework and the EU AI Act
Tool-neutral by design This guide names no products, prices, limits or rankings, because they change quickly. Prompts shown are examples to adapt, and results will vary between assistants.

How assistants behave, in practice

An AI assistant generates text in response to your prompt and whatever context you provide. Three practical consequences follow.

BehaviourWhat it means for you
It produces fluent text whether or not it is rightErrors and invented details, often called hallucinations, can sound confident. Check what matters.
It only knows what the prompt and its tools give itProvide the context it needs, such as the audience, the source text or the policy.
Results vary between tries and between toolsReview every output. A prompt that worked last time may not give the same answer.

Writing prompts that work

Google's prompting guide recommends clear and specific instructions, context, a stated response format, and examples. Anthropic's overview lists clarity, examples, structure, role prompting, thinking and prompt chaining as the main techniques. A practical checklist:

TaskWhat exactly should it produce?
ContextWhat does it need to know: the source text, the situation, the constraints?
Audience and toneWho will read it, and how should it sound?
Format and lengthList, table, email, three paragraphs, under 100 words?
ExampleIs there a sample of the style you want? Keep example formats consistent.

Iterating on the answer

The first answer is a draft. Google's guide notes that prompt design can need a few iterations, and suggests rephrasing, reordering content and adding detail. Anthropic's guide adds that, before tuning a prompt, you should know what success looks like and have a way to test it.

  • Name what is wrong. "Too formal, and it ignores the deadline" works better than "try again".
  • Add what was missing. Context, constraints, or an example of the style you want.
  • Split big tasks. Prompt chaining means one output feeds the next, such as outline, then draft, then edit. Each stage is easier to check.
  • Save prompts that work, and re-check their output each time.

Checking the output

You remain responsible for what you use. A confident tone is not evidence.

Output typeHow to check it
Facts and claimsConfirm against a reliable primary source
Numbers and totalsRecompute with a spreadsheet or calculator
References and linksOpen them: do they exist, and do they say what is claimed?
QuotesFind the original; do not rely on the assistant's wording
SummariesCompare with the source for anything omitted or distorted
Formulas and codeTest on sample data, including awkward cases
Not independent Asking the same assistant "are you sure?" is not verification. Use a source or a tool outside the assistant.

Privacy and responsible use

  • Check policy first. Your employer or client may restrict which tools you can use and what you can share. Rules differ, so ask if unsure.
  • Share the minimum. Remove names, account numbers and other identifying details where you can.
  • Keep secrets out. Do not paste passwords, access keys or confidential material into tools that are not approved for it.
  • Own the result. You are accountable for the work you send. Check rules on AI use and disclosure for the context you work in.
  • Be careful with copyright and legal questions. This guide is not legal advice; check current law and your organisation's guidance.

Practical learning exercises

Outputs vary between assistants, so each exercise has a check you can apply to your own result.

  • Exercise 1 (prompt rewrite). Rewrite "Write a post about our sale" so it states the task, audience, tone, length and format. Check: a stranger could tell exactly what to produce from your prompt.
  • Exercise 2 (iterate). Get a first draft, then write one follow-up that names what is wrong and adds what was missing. Check: the second draft fixes the named problem without losing what was good.
  • Exercise 3 (fact check). Ask for three facts about a subject you know well, then verify each against a reliable source. Check: record which were right, which were wrong, and which you could not confirm.
  • Exercise 4 (numbers). Give the assistant six numbers and ask for the total and the average. Recompute them in a spreadsheet. Check: do they match?
  • Exercise 5 (privacy). Take a real work paragraph with names and figures and rewrite it so it could be shared safely, keeping the meaning. Check: no identifying details remain, and the question is still answerable.

Interview questions

Try answering aloud before you open each one.

How do you write a good prompt?

State the task, give the context the assistant needs, say who the output is for, and specify the format and constraints such as length and tone. Add an example when you want a particular style. Google's prompting guide recommends clear, specific instructions, context, a response format and examples.

What should you do when the first answer is not good enough?

Treat it as a draft and refine the prompt: add missing context, tighten the constraints, reorder or rephrase the request, or show an example. Prompt design often needs a few iterations.

Why can an AI assistant be confidently wrong?

Assistants generate fluent text, and fluent text can contain errors or invented details, often called hallucinations. A confident tone says nothing about accuracy, so important facts, figures, quotes and references need checking against reliable sources.

How do you check AI output?

Verify facts and figures against primary sources, open any cited link and confirm it says what is claimed, recompute numbers with a tool you trust, and compare summaries with the original. Check anything that would be costly if wrong.

What is prompt chaining and why use it?

Splitting a large task into steps, where one output feeds the next. Each step is simpler to specify and to check, and you can correct course early.

What data should you not put into an AI assistant?

Personal data, client or company confidential information, credentials and anything your organisation has not approved for the tool. Follow your employer's policy, share the minimum needed, and remove identifying details where you can.

Who is responsible for AI-assisted work?

The person who uses and sends it. You are accountable for what it says, so check it, and follow your organisation's or client's rules on AI use and disclosure.

How would you decide whether a task suits AI?

It suits tasks where a draft helps and you can verify the result: summarising, rewriting, brainstorming, explaining, formatting. It suits tasks less when an unnoticed error is costly and you cannot easily check it.

How do you measure whether a prompt is working?

Define success criteria first, for example accuracy, format and tone, then test the prompt on several realistic inputs and compare results against those criteria. Anthropic's guide assumes success criteria and a way to test them before prompt tuning.

What is the NIST AI Risk Management Framework?

A voluntary framework from NIST, released as version 1.0 on 26 January 2023, to help organisations manage AI risks. Its core functions are Govern, Map, Measure and Manage. NIST also published a Generative AI Profile (NIST-AI-600-1) in July 2024.

What is the EU AI Act in one sentence?

A risk-based law: some AI practices are banned, high-risk uses such as CV-scanning tools face specific legal requirements, and most other applications are largely unregulated. Application dates are staged and being updated, so check current information.

Why might the same prompt give different answers on different tries?

Assistants generate text with some variation, and different tools and versions behave differently. Save prompts that work, but always review each output rather than assuming it will match last time.

Practice questions

Question 1 of 20Prompting

Which of these prompts is most likely to get a useful first draft?

Why: It states the task, audience, length, tone and format. Google's prompting guide recommends clear, specific instructions, including constraints such as length.
Question 2 of 20Prompting

Google's prompting guide recommends including examples in a prompt. What should you keep consistent in them?

Why: Few-shot examples show the pattern you want. If the formats differ from each other, the output format can drift too.
Question 3 of 20Prompting

Why supply reference material, such as a policy or a customer email, in your prompt?

Why: Context helps the assistant understand the details of what you are asking. It does not remove your duty to check the result.
Question 4 of 20Iterating

The first answer misses the point. What is the best next step?

Why: Prompt design often needs a few iterations. Google's guide suggests rephrasing, reordering content and adding detail, then checking the results.
Question 5 of 20Iterating

Anthropic's guide assumes you have three things before you tune a prompt. Which is one of them?

Why: The three are: success criteria, a way to test against them, and a first draft prompt. Without a definition of good, you cannot tell whether a prompt change helped.
Question 6 of 20Iterating

What is prompt chaining?

Why: Splitting a large task into stages also makes each stage easier to check.
Question 7 of 20Checking

An assistant states a statistic confidently and cites no source. What should you do before using it?

Why: Fluent, confident wording is not evidence. Asking the assistant to confirm its own answer is not independent verification.
Question 8 of 20Checking

What is a hallucination in this context?

Why: Assistants can produce confident text that is inaccurate, including invented facts, quotes or references. That is why output needs checking.
Question 9 of 20Checking

An assistant gives you a reference with a link. What is the safest check?

Why: Invented or misdescribed references are a known failure. Always read the source.
Question 10 of 20Checking

An assistant totals a column of figures for a report. What should you do?

Why: Treat numbers as unverified until you recompute them with a tool you trust.
Question 11 of 20Checking

You ask an assistant to summarise a long report. What should you check in the summary?

Why: A summary can read well and still omit key caveats or change a meaning. Compare it with the original for anything that matters.
Question 12 of 20Privacy

You want to paste a customer list with names and emails into a public AI assistant to tidy it. What should you do first?

Why: Personal and confidential data should only go into tools your organisation has approved for that purpose. Policies differ, so check yours.
Question 13 of 20Privacy

Which is the best practice when a task involves confidential company information?

Why: Data minimisation reduces exposure. The assistant cannot reliably tell you what your employer allows.
Question 14 of 20Responsible use

You use AI to help draft a client deliverable. What should you check before sending it?

Why: Rules about AI use and disclosure differ between organisations and contracts. The person who sends the work is accountable for what it says.
Question 15 of 20NIST

How does NIST describe the AI Risk Management Framework's use?

Why: NIST says the AI RMF is intended for voluntary use, to help manage AI risks to individuals, organisations and society.
Question 16 of 20NIST

Which list gives the four core functions of the NIST AI RMF?

Why: The AI RMF's core functions are Govern, Map, Measure and Manage.
Question 17 of 20NIST

What is NIST-AI-600-1?

Why: It is the Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework: Generative Artificial Intelligence Profile, which helps organisations identify risks that are unique to generative AI.
Question 18 of 20EU AI Act

What is the EU AI Act's general approach?

Why: The Act sorts AI uses by risk. Unacceptable-risk applications are banned, high-risk ones face specific legal requirements, and the rest are largely left unregulated.
Question 19 of 20EU AI Act

Which example does the AI Act information site give of a high-risk application?

Why: Hiring tools affect people's opportunities, so they are an example of a high-risk use with specific legal requirements. Check current dates and rules, because they are being updated.
Question 20 of 20Workflow

You need an assistant's help on a long, multi-part report. Which approach is generally better?

Why: Stages are easier to steer and check. You can correct direction early instead of rewriting a finished draft.

Preparation checklist

  • Rewrite a vague prompt so it has task, context, audience, format and constraints
  • Describe how you would iterate on a weak first answer
  • Explain why a confident tone is not evidence, and list four ways to check output
  • Explain prompt chaining and when to use it
  • State what you would not paste into an AI tool, and where you would check policy
  • Describe the NIST AI RMF (voluntary; Govern, Map, Measure, Manage) and the EU AI Act's risk-based approach
  • Check the linked official pages for current details before quoting dates or rules
