Getting Started

LinkedIn Skill Assessments Are Retired: What to Do Instead

11 min read Published Oct 18, 2026 Reviewed Oct 18, 2026

Unofficial study resource. Written independently of any exam provider, employer or software vendor.

If you landed here looking for LinkedIn Skill Assessment answers or study material: LinkedIn's Help Center now lists Skill Assessments as no longer available. SkillPrep started life as a study site for those assessments, so we have rebuilt it around the skills themselves — Excel, Python, SQL, JavaScript, Git and more — which matter whether or not a badge exists.

What happened to Skill Assessments?

LinkedIn Skill Assessments were short multiple-choice quizzes that earned a skill badge on your profile. LinkedIn has since retired the feature, and its Help Center page on the topic is titled "Skill Assessments - no longer available".

Because LinkedIn controls that feature and may update its details, check the official page (linked under Sources) for the current position on existing badges and your data rather than relying on third-party summaries — including this one.

Be careful with old material Any site still promising "latest LinkedIn Skill Assessment answers" is selling something that can no longer be taken. Rules like retake waiting periods or badge visibility settings no longer apply.

What this means for you

  • You cannot earn a new assessment badge, so there is nothing to "pass" on LinkedIn.
  • The underlying skills are unchanged. Recruiters and interviewers still test Excel, SQL, Python and the rest directly.
  • Evidence of what you can do now carries more weight than a quiz score: projects, work samples, recognised certifications and clear descriptions of results.

Better ways to show your skills

SkillEvidence that works

Pick two or three that match the roles you want. Quality beats quantity — a handful of relevant, checkable items outweighs a long list of generic ones.

Why a retired quiz badge was never the real goal

The LinkedIn Skill Assessments feature is retired, so there is no badge left to chase. That is less of a loss than it sounds. A short multiple-choice quiz only ever showed that you could recognise correct answers on a given day. It did not show that you could finish a task, work with other people, or fix something that went wrong. Hiring managers know the difference, which is why the most persuasive proof of skill has always been evidence of work: something a stranger can look at, check, and judge for themselves.

Good evidence has three qualities. It is specific: it names the tool, the task and the scale. It is verifiable: someone can open it, run it, read it, or phone a person who saw you do it. And it is recent enough to be believable: a skill you used last month is more convincing than one you list from a course years ago.

Think of every skill you claim as a small argument. The claim is "I can analyse data in a spreadsheet". The evidence is a workbook with a clear question, clean inputs, a pivot table, a chart and two sentences on what you concluded. The strongest claims carry two or three pieces of evidence of different kinds, such as a project, a result at work, and a reference.

Evidence also needs to hang together. If your profile claims advanced analysis skills but your projects only show basic tables, a careful reader will notice the gap. It is better to describe yourself accurately at a lower level and show steady growth than to overclaim and be caught out. Honest scope, such as "intermediate, used daily for two years", reads as confident rather than weak, and it gives the interviewer a fair starting point for questions.

Rule of thumb For each skill you list, be able to answer "Show me" in under a minute. If you cannot, either build the evidence or take the skill off the list.

Build a small portfolio that proves skills

A portfolio is simply a collection of finished work with context. It is not only for designers and developers. Analysts, marketers, project coordinators, writers, teachers and operations staff can all show work. Aim for three to five pieces rather than twenty; quality and clarity beat volume.

Choose pieces that match the jobs you want, not everything you have ever done. For each one, write a short case note with the same simple shape:

  1. Problem: what needed solving and for whom, in one or two sentences.
  2. Approach: the steps you took and why you chose them over alternatives.
  3. Tools: the software, methods or frameworks you used.
  4. Result: what changed, with a number where you honestly have one, or a clear qualitative outcome where you do not.
  5. Lessons: what you would do differently next time. This line shows judgement.

Where the work is confidential, rebuild a similar example with public or invented data and say so plainly. Never publish employer data without permission. For code, keep a tidy repository with a readable README. For writing, publish samples somewhere stable. For analysis, share the workbook or a short slide deck. For project or operations work, a one-page summary of the plan, the risks and the outcome is enough.

Make each piece easy to check. Include dates, link to the finished artefact if it can be shared publicly, and keep file names and descriptions honest. A small, accurate portfolio that a reviewer can open in two minutes does more than a large one that is vague.

Tip Ask a colleague, mentor or user of your work for a one-line comment you may quote, with their permission. A named third-party remark is strong evidence.

Write your About section and experience bullets with outcomes

Profiles and CVs usually fail in the same way: they list duties instead of results. "Responsible for reporting" says where you sat. It does not say whether you were good. Rewrite each bullet so it answers three questions: what did you do, how, and what happened because of it?

A reliable pattern is: action verb + what you did + method or tool + outcome. Compare the weak and strong versions below. The figures are placeholders; use only numbers you can defend.

Duty-style bulletOutcome-style bullet
Managed weekly sales reportsAutomated the weekly sales report using spreadsheet formulas and a pivot table, cutting preparation from half a day to under an hour
Handled customer emailsWrote a set of reply templates for the five most common queries, which helped the team answer faster and more consistently
Helped with a website redesignRewrote product pages and tested two headline versions; the clearer version received more clicks to the order page

If you lack a number, describe the change in plain words: faster, fewer errors, clearer, cheaper, safer. Do not invent or inflate figures; interviewers often ask how you measured them.

For the About section, keep it short and human. A good structure is three or four sentences: who you are and what you do, the kind of problems you are good at solving, one or two proof points, and what you are looking for next. Write in the first person, avoid buzzwords such as "passionate" and "results-driven" unless a fact follows them, and place your strongest evidence early.

List skills that you can actually demonstrate, and order them by relevance to the role you want. Match the wording of the job advertisements you are targeting, since recruiters and systems often search by those terms, but only where it is true.

Choose certifications sensibly

A certification is one kind of evidence, not a substitute for the others. It can be valuable when it is recognised by employers in your field, tested under supervised or standardised conditions, and tied to a skill that job advertisements actually request. It adds little when it is easy to obtain, unknown to the people reading your CV, or unrelated to your target role.

Before paying for anything, run a short check:

  • Demand: read ten job advertisements for your target role. Which credentials appear repeatedly? Treat those as candidates.
  • Issuer: is it awarded by a professional body, a software vendor for its own product, or a respected training institution? Can an employer check it?
  • Assessment: does it require a proctored exam or a practical project, or just attendance?
  • Cost and renewal: include exam fees, study time, and any renewal or continuing education requirements.
  • Fit with experience: some credentials expect prior work experience; others are entry level. Pick the one that matches where you are.

Pair every certification with a project that uses the same skill. If you earn a data analysis credential, publish a short analysis of a public dataset. If you earn a project management credential, write up how you applied planning and risk techniques to a real task. The credential gets you noticed; the project convinces the reader.

Beware Do not list a certification you have not completed, and do not describe course attendance as certification. Claims like this are easy to check and can cost you an offer.

A 30-day skill-building plan

Thirty days will not make you an expert, but it is enough to produce one credible piece of evidence and to start building a habit. Choose one target skill tied to a role you want, and plan roughly 45 to 60 minutes on weekdays.

DaysFocusOutput
1–3Pick the skill and read job advertisements; list the five tasks the role performs mostOne-page skill map
4–10Learn the fundamentals from one good course or book; practise daily with small exercisesNotes and 5 to 7 practice exercises
11–20Build a real mini-project that solves a genuine problem, ideally for a real person or small organisationWorking draft of the project
21–25Get feedback from someone experienced, then improve; document your approach and resultsFinished project and case note
26–28Update your profile or CV bullets and About section using the case noteRevised profile with an outcome bullet
29–30Rehearse two STAR stories about the project aloud; share the work with your networkTwo practised stories

Keep a simple log of what you did each day. It keeps you honest and gives you dates and details to quote later. If you miss a day, continue; do not restart. Repeat the cycle with a second skill once the first has produced a finished piece.

Protect the plan from the usual failure modes. Do not spend the whole month watching lessons without producing anything; passive learning feels productive but leaves little to show. Do not choose a project so large that it cannot be finished; a small, complete piece beats an ambitious abandoned one. And do not learn in isolation: even one conversation with a practitioner can save days of wrong turns.

Talking about skills in interviews, and what employers look at

Interviewers rarely want a list of skills. They want proof that you have used them under real conditions. The STAR format keeps your answers structured and short, usually around two minutes:

  • Situation: the context, briefly. Where were you and what was at stake?
  • Task: your specific responsibility in that situation.
  • Action: what you did, step by step. Spend most of your time here and say "I" for your own actions, even if the project was a team effort.
  • Result: the outcome, a measurable one if possible, plus what you learned.

Example: "Our team's monthly report kept arriving with mismatched totals (situation). I was asked to find the cause and fix it (task). I compared the source files, found that two branches used different date formats, wrote a short cleaning step in the spreadsheet and added a checklist for submitters (action). Totals matched from the next cycle, and the report went out a day earlier (result)." Notice that it names the problem, the actions and the effect without exaggeration.

Prepare four to six stories that you can adapt: a problem you solved, a mistake you fixed, a disagreement you handled, something you learned quickly, and a time you led or helped others. Practise saying them aloud and trim the detail the listener does not need.

When employers evaluate you they typically look across several signals: relevant evidence such as projects and results; clarity of communication in your application and answers; consistency between your CV, profile and what you say; references or third-party comments; learning ability, shown by how you picked up new tools; and fit with the role, shown by examples that resemble the actual work. Honesty matters most. If asked about something you only know a little, say what you know, what you have tried, and how you would learn the rest. That answer is far stronger than a bluff.

Before you apply Pick three skills from the job description, and for each have one project, one STAR story and one line of profile text ready.
